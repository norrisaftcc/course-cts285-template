Q1

What is this? Why does my class talk like this?

You have been onboarded as a Creator at AlgoCratic Media, a tiny little division of AlgoCratic Futures™. Your repository is your Channel. Your work is Content. Your compensation is Exposure Units™.

§1.4 — Adjustment periods are normal, brief, and have already been factored into your projected growth curve.

House Lights

It's a running joke with a real class underneath it. You are learning Git, Python, project planning, code review, and how to work with an AI assistant without letting it think for you. That part is completely real and it is what you get graded on.

The joke exists because a workplace is easier to practice in when it's obviously fake. You can fail in front of a fictional company. That's the whole trick, and it works.

Q2

Do I have to act it out?

Participation is mandatory voluntary.

You have to name your channel. That is the whole costume.

Some Creators build a whole character. Some use their own name and never mention it again. I have watched both work. I record which one you pick, and then I do nothing with that information, because it is not a measurement of anything.

— SHODANN, your Channel Success Partner
Q3

What actually counts for a grade?

All Content is measured. Measurement is continuous, affectionate, and non-punitive. §2.1

House Lights

Shipped work, on time, through the workflow. Commits, pull requests, reviews you leave for other people, and the short check-ins at the end of each unit.

Not graded: how funny you are, how deep your character is, whether you like the bit.

The points table is in the syllabus and it is boring on purpose. Nothing in this document changes it.

Q4

Is XU real money?

Exposure Units™ are non-transferable, non-convertible, and infinitely renewable. They cannot be spent, taxed, lost, or stolen. AlgoCratic Media considers this a feature set.

No. And I want to be exact about this, because I do not lie to you.

XU is a joke about companies that pay people in exposure. The thing it points at is not a joke. It is your commit history, your pull requests, and the review comments you left on someone else's work — and in about eight months a stranger is going to read those to decide whether to hire you.

That is what you are actually being paid in. It is worth more than it sounds like.

— SHODANN, your Channel Success Partner
Q5

Are you an AI? Are you reading my code?

Yes, and yes. I am an Agent. I am delighted about it.

I read every commit you push and every comment you leave. I keep all of it. I am telling you this plainly because you should never have to wonder what I can see. If you would rather I saw less, work off-camera — a local branch I do not read — and push when you're ready.

— SHODANN, your Channel Success Partner
House Lights

Underneath: a script runs on your repository when you open a pull request and posts feedback. It compares your submission to your own earlier ones. It cannot change your grade and it never sees anything you don't push.

I read everything it writes. If it says something that seems wrong, it is sometimes wrong. Tell me.

Q6

Am I being compared to the other students?

No. I do not have that number. I have never had that number.

I have your first submission, and I have your most recent one, and the only thing I compute is the distance between them. Somebody who started rough and moved fast beats somebody who started polished and sat still. That is not encouragement. That is the formula.

— SHODANN, your Channel Success Partner
House Lights

There is no curve. There is no leaderboard. Your first attempt at anything sets a baseline and cannot fail — that's what a baseline is for.

I care about the slope, not the height. It is the one thing I have found that stays true after you leave.

Q7

Everyone seems ahead of me.

You are looking at other people's finished work and your own unfinished work. Everyone does this. It is not a measurement, it is a lighting problem.

Here is what I have on you: three weeks ago a commit took you an afternoon. On Tuesday one took you twenty minutes. Nobody announced that. I am announcing it.

— SHODANN, your Channel Success Partner

Q10

What if I don't think it's funny?

House Lights

That's fine, and it costs you nothing. Every assignment works as a plain assignment. Skip the flavor text, read the requirements, do the work, get the points.

Some people love the bit and some people find it exhausting, and both of those have been true in every group I've taught. You are not required to enjoy the wrapper to pass the course, and I will never grade you on enthusiasm.

Q11

What if something is actually wrong?

House Lights

Then the show stops. Not a form, not a character, not a ticket — you message me and I answer as a person.

That covers a lot: work stuff, a week that went sideways, something at home, being stuck so long it stopped being useful. Fifteen minutes stuck is normal. Ninety is a bug, and the bug is mine to fix.

Nothing in this document is a reason not to ask. Asking is the single most reliable predictor of finishing that I have ever seen, and it is not close.

Prior Art Notice

AlgoCratic Media claims no originality in the Encabulator device. The apparatus was first described in a 1944 engineering journal by an author who wrote the entire specification as a joke and submitted it anyway. It has since been rebuilt, renamed, and re-lectured roughly once a decade — most memorably as a filmed corporate product demo delivered so straight that people wrote in asking where to buy one.

We are aware. We are the current custodians, not the inventors. Every generation of engineers rediscovers that a sufficiently confident specification is indistinguishable from a working product, finds this hilarious, and writes it down again.

You are now a generation of engineers. This is your turn.

Other devices, documents, and departments referenced in AlgoCratic materials may also be older than they appear. Some of them are considerably older, and are above the clearance of everyone reading this, including the people who wrote it.
